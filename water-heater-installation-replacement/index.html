Repair Or Replace

When Replacement Makes More Sense Than Repair

A water heater that leaks from the tank body cannot be patched. Once the steel liner corrodes through, the unit is finished, and every day it stays connected risks a flooded closet or garage. Leaks at a fitting, the relief valve or a drain valve are different, and those often come down to a part.

Age is the other deciding factor. The U.S. Department of Energy puts typical tank water heater life at 10 to 15 years and tankless units at 20 years or more. A 13-year-old tank with a failed thermostat is rarely worth the repair bill, while a 5-year-old unit with a bad element is. The page on emergency water heater repair covers the problems that are worth fixing.

Warning Signs

Signs Your Water Heater Is Ready For Replacement

Water around the base

Moisture under the tank usually means the shell is leaking, not a loose fitting.

Rust-colored hot water

If only the hot taps run brown, the inside of the tank is corroding.

Rumbling or popping

Sediment on the tank floor traps steam bubbles that burst as the burner or element heats.

Past 12 years old

Check the serial number sticker. Many manufacturers encode the build date in it.

Repeat failures

A second heating element, thermostat or igniter in a few years points to a tired unit.

Not enough hot water

A household that has grown may simply have outgrown a 30 or 40 gallon tank.

Local Water

What High Point's Soft Water Means For A New Heater

The city's annual water quality reports, published from 2016 through 2019, listed hardness at roughly 30 to 60 mg/L, which is soft. Scale builds slowly in that kind of water, so a tank here is more likely to fail from age and a spent anode rod than from a crust of limescale.

A new tank still collects fine sediment, so most manufacturers recommend flushing it about once a year. An anode rod check around year five is cheap and can add years to the tank. Because the water is already soft, adding a salt-based softener for the sake of the heater is rarely worth it.

Sizing And Code

Sizing, Venting And What The Code Requires

Sizing starts with the first-hour rating, the number of gallons a tank can deliver in its first hour of heavy use. A household of four with two bathrooms often lands on a 50 gallon tank, but a long run of morning showers can justify more. A plumber should ask about your peak hour before recommending a size.

Gas units need correct venting and combustion air, and every installation needs a temperature and pressure relief valve piped to a safe discharge point. A closed water system, which is common once a pressure reducing valve or backflow preventer is installed, also needs a thermal expansion tank under the plumbing code.

Federal efficiency rules in place since April 2015 mean most electric tanks above 55 gallons are now heat pump models. An old 80 gallon electric unit cannot always be swapped like for like. Work in North Carolina follows the NC Plumbing Code, and the installer should confirm permit requirements with the local inspections office before starting.

Local Housing

Where High Point Heaters Live, And Why It Matters

In a 1940s to 1980s south-side ranch the heater often sits in a garage or a small utility closet. In a mill house near Highland Cotton Mills it may be wedged into a crawlspace or a tight back room. Access changes the labor, and a 50 gallon tank does not always fit through a narrow door or hatch.

Older Uptown Suburbs houses, built between 1903 and 1963, sometimes have heaters added decades after the house was built, with supply lines of several different metals meeting at the tank. Mixed metals corrode at the joints, so a good installer replaces the connectors rather than reusing them. If the supply lines themselves look tired, whole-home repiping is worth pricing at the same visit.
