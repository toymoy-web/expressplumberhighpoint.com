Decision

Repair Or Replace The Sewer Line

Repair is enough when

  • The camera shows one cracked or offset section
  • The rest of the pipe is round and in line
  • Roots entered at a single joint
  • The pipe material is still sound

Replace when

  • The pipe has collapsed or belly-sagged for a long stretch
  • Cracks or open joints repeat along the line
  • The material is failing, such as Orangeburg or crushed clay
  • Repairs keep adding up
Why Replace

What A Full Replacement Fixes

Replacement swaps the entire run from the house to the connection with the municipal main or septic tank. New plastic pipe has smooth walls, tight joints and a continuous slope, which stops root intrusion and reduces clogs. It also removes the guesswork of repeated spot repairs on pipe that is failing everywhere.

It is a bigger project than repair, and the camera inspection is what tells you whether you need it. If the video shows only a short problem, ask about sewer repair first. If it shows a broken line from end to end, replacement is the honest recommendation.

Older Neighborhoods

What Gets Replaced In Older High Point Streets

Clay tile laterals are common under houses built before the 1960s, with roots working into their many joints. Some mid-century houses have bituminized fiber pipe, which softens and collapses over time. Ranches built from the 1940s through the 1980s may have cast iron under the slab, which rusts through eventually.

Emerywood's old trees and the Uptown Suburbs' mature landscaping make trenchless replacement attractive there. It can preserve a historic yard that open-trench would tear up. A mill house near Highland Cotton Mills often has a short run to a nearby main, which can keep the job simple.

Code And Slope

What The Plumbing Code Expects

The NC Plumbing Code is based on the International Plumbing Code (IPC). Drain lines need a continuous downward slope, commonly one-eighth to one-quarter of an inch per foot depending on pipe size, and cleanouts at accessible intervals. NC811 must be called before digging, and an inspection is usually required before the trench is covered.

A new cleanout is worth installing if the old line had none. It costs little during replacement and saves money every time the line needs service later.

Before It Starts

Getting Ready For A Replacement

Buried utilities are marked before anyone digs. In North Carolina the NC811 service marks public utility lines, and the call is made ahead of excavation. Private lines such as irrigation, a shed circuit or a well line are usually not marked, so tell the crew what you know.

Plan for access and for water. Trenchless work needs a small pit at each end, while open-cut work needs a trench along the route. The household usually has no sewer service while the old line is out, so ask how long that window is.

Pipe Choice

What The New Line Is Made Of

Most residential replacements use PVC or a similar plastic pipe. It resists corrosion and roots when the joints are sealed and the pipe is bedded and sloped correctly. The old cast iron and clay lines it replaces are what fail in many older neighborhoods.

Ask which material and which diameter are being installed, and whether a cleanout is added. A new cleanout at the house makes every later camera check and cleaning easier.

After The Work

Testing, Footage And Restoring The Yard

Expect a test before the site is restored. A camera pass and a water or air test show that the joints hold and the slope is right. Ask for a copy of the final footage and keep it with the house records.

Restoration depends on the method. Trenchless work leaves two small patches, while open-cut work leaves a trench to backfill, compact and reseed or repave. Ask who restores the lawn or driveway and when.

Comparing Quotes

How To Compare Written Estimates

Compare estimates on scope, not only on price. Check the length of pipe, the method, the pipe material, the restoration included and whether the permit and inspection are in the price. A lower bid that leaves out restoration or the permit is not a lower price.

Ask how the crew handles a surprise, such as a second break or a mass of roots that the camera did not show. The estimate should say whether a change in scope is quoted before extra work begins.
