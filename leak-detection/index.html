DIY Test

How To Check For A Hidden Leak Yourself

Two simple tests tell you whether water is escaping before you pay for anything.

  1. 1

    Turn off every fixture and appliance that uses water, including the ice maker and the irrigation timer.

  2. 2

    Find the water meter, usually in a box near the street, and look at the leak indicator. It is a small dial or triangle that spins when water moves.

  3. 3

    If the dial spins with everything off, or the digits change over 30 minutes, water is leaking somewhere between the meter and the fixtures.

  4. 4

    Next, add a few drops of food coloring to each toilet tank. If color reaches the bowl within 10 minutes without flushing, that toilet is leaking.

  5. 5

    Compare your winter bills. For a family of four, use above 12,000 gallons a month in winter suggests a leak.

Emergency Leaks

When A Hidden Leak Becomes An Emergency

A hidden leak turns into an emergency when water is visibly spreading, when the meter keeps turning with every fixture off or when a wall, ceiling or floor feels wet. Close the main valve first, then call the emergency line. A plumber can locate the leak while the water is off, and the damage stops growing.

People describe three situations most often. The meter will not stop moving, a stain is spreading across a ceiling, or part of the floor feels warm or soggy. Each one points to a different test, so say exactly what you see and where. The dispatcher uses that to send the right equipment on the first trip.

What Leaks Cost

Small Leaks Add Up Quickly

According to the EPA's WaterSense program, the average household loses more than 10,000 gallons a year to leaks, and one home in ten loses 90 gallons or more every day. Fixing easily corrected leaks can trim about 10 percent from a water bill.

Those figures are for ordinary drips and running toilets. A pinhole in a pressurized line under a slab can leak much more and also damage flooring, subfloor and framing over months. Finding it early costs far less than finding it after the floor warps.

Local Housing

Where Leaks Hide In High Point Homes

South-side ranches built from the 1940s through the 1980s often have supply lines in or under a concrete slab, and cast-iron drains below that. A slab leak in one of these houses may show only as a warm patch or a spinning meter. When the cause is confirmed under the floor, slab leak detection covers the next step.

In mill houses near Highland Cotton Mills and in the Uptown Suburbs, galvanized supply lines corrode from the inside and pinhole at the joints. Crawlspaces hide those drips for years. Polybutylene tubing in homes from roughly 1978 to 1995 adds another leak risk. Pinpointing the leak first lets you decide between a spot repair and a full repipe project.

Honest Limits

What Leak Detection Can And Cannot Do

Detection can

  • Confirm that a leak exists
  • Narrow it to a line, room or few feet
  • Reduce how much wall or floor is opened
  • Document the leak for an insurance claim

Detection cannot

  • Find every leak on the first pass in all conditions
  • Locate a leak without any water pressure on the line
  • Repair the pipe by itself
  • Guarantee a result in very noisy or deep installations

The Written Report

What A Good Leak Report Should Say

A useful report names the location of the leak, the pipe it is in and the method that found it. It separates a supply leak from a drain leak, because the repairs differ. Moisture readings or photos should back up the finding.

If the leak is under a slab, behind a wall or in a yard, the report explains the access needed and which tests ruled out other lines. Some homeowner policies cover sudden water damage or the cost of finding a leak, while slow leaks are often excluded, so keep the report and photos.

Before The Visit

What To Do While You Wait For A Visit

Close the shutoff valve to any fixture you suspect and note which sounds or stains come and go. A drip that stops when one valve is closed narrows the search to that branch. Photograph any stain with a coin or tape measure beside it, so changes over a few days are easy to see.

Do not open walls or floors yourself. A cut in the wrong place adds repair cost and removes the evidence the tools are trying to read.

Water Bill And Meter

Using The Meter To Prove A Leak Before You Pay For A Search

The most common reason people call about leaks is a water bill that has crept up or a meter that still turns after everything is off. Both are useful clues. Read the meter, shut off every fixture, wait twenty minutes and read it again. Any movement means water is leaving somewhere.

Close the house valve and check again to split the problem in two. If the meter stops, the leak is inside the house. If it keeps moving, the leak sits between the meter and the house, which is a job for water line repair. Write both readings down for the plumber.

Ceilings And Walls

A Stain On The Ceiling Or Water Behind A Wall

A ceiling stain rarely sits under its source. Water travels along framing and pipe before it shows, so the wet spot can be several feet from the leak. Bathrooms above, a supply line in the wall and a roof or HVAC drain can all produce the same brown ring.

Thermal imaging and a moisture meter trace the wet path back to its start without opening everything. Mark the edge of the stain with a pencil and the date so growth is easy to see. A stain that spreads in a day is an emergency, and one that is dry and old may be a past leak that already stopped.

Under The House

Leaks In The Crawlspace And Under The Floor

Many High Point houses sit on a crawlspace, and a leak there can run for months because nobody looks. Signs include a musty smell, sagging insulation, a floor that feels soft near a bathroom and a meter that moves with the fixtures off. Wet dirt under one spot is often the first visible clue.

A plumber crawls the space with a light, checks supply lines, drain joints and the water heater connections and uses sound or pressure tests where a pipe cannot be seen. If the crawlspace is wet only after rain, the water may be coming from outside instead, which the guide on a wet crawlspace after rain explains.

Yards And Irrigation

Yard, Irrigation And Meter-Box Leaks

Outside leaks show up as a soggy patch, a spot of unusually green grass or a meter box that is always full of water. Irrigation lines, hose bibs and the line to a detached building are the usual suspects. Shutting off the irrigation system and rechecking the meter separates a sprinkler leak from a house leak.

Pools and spas have their own plumbing and loss patterns, so ask the dispatcher up front whether the plumber covers them. A leak in the meter box itself usually belongs to the water utility, so check whose side it is on before you pay for a repair.
