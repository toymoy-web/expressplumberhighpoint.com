Plumbing Permits In North Carolina: When Does A Job Need One?

Some plumbing jobs need a permit and some do not. State law sets exemptions, and your local office applies the rest.

The Short Answer

It Depends On The Job, The Cost And The Local Office

North Carolina law exempts some small residential jobs from building permits. According to S.L. 2016-113, Section 13, no permit is needed for work costing $15,000 or less in a single-family home if it follows the current State Building Code, unless it involves things like load-bearing structure or a change in the design of plumbing. Plumbing replacements that do not change size or capacity are among the exempt items.

The same law exempts replacing a water heater in a one- or two-family home when the energy input is not greater, there is no change in fuel, location, venting or piping, and a licensed plumbing contractor does the work. Local inspections offices still administer the rules, so always confirm.

Who Decides

Local Inspections Offices Apply The Rules

High Point, Jamestown, Archdale, Trinity, Thomasville, Randleman and Greensboro each work with their own city or county inspections office, and addresses on county lines can fall under different ones. The state sets the framework, and the local office issues permits and inspections. A call to the right office before work starts is free.

A plumber should know the local process and pull any needed permit in their own name. If a plumber says no permit is needed, ask why. If they say one is needed, ask who is pulling it.

Protect Yourself

What To Do Before You Approve Plumbing Work

A few questions prevent problems at sale or insurance time.

  1. 1

    Ask whether the job needs a permit and who will obtain it.

  2. 2

    Confirm the plumber holds a North Carolina license through the state board's public lookup.

  3. 3

    Ask for the permit number and keep it with your records.

  4. 4

    Schedule any required inspection before walls or trenches are covered.

  5. 5

    Keep the final inspection record for your insurance and for future buyers.

Why It Matters

Unpermitted Work Can Cause Trouble Later

Unpermitted work that should have had a permit can complicate a home sale, an insurance claim or a mortgage appraisal. It can also hide mistakes that an inspection would have caught, such as improper venting on a gas water heater. A permit and inspection protect the owner as well as the building. For specific jobs, see new water heater installation, repiping service and gas piping repair.

Local

County Lines Matter

A parcel near Randleman may fall under Randolph County or a neighboring county's office, and a ZIP code can span several communities. Use the parcel address, not the mailing town, when you call.

Inspections

What An Inspection Usually Checks

Plumbing inspections often happen in stages. A rough-in inspection looks at pipes before walls close, a pressure or leak test checks the system, and a final inspection confirms fixtures, venting and safety devices. For a water heater, the inspector looks at the relief valve and its discharge pipe, the venting for gas units and the connections.

Work has to be accessible when the inspector arrives. Plan the schedule so the inspection happens before walls, trenches or insulation cover the work.

Money And Time

Fees, Timing And Doing Your Own Work

Permit fees vary by jurisdiction and by the size or value of the job, and scheduling an inspection can add a few days. Ask your plumber how those fit into the schedule. Licensed contractors usually include the permit and inspection in the quote or list them separately.

In many places an owner can pull a permit for work on their own home, within limits. Rules differ, and a licensed plumber is required for some types of work. Ask your local inspections office before you start.

Make The Call

What To Ask The Inspections Office

A short call settles most questions. Give the parcel address, describe the job in plain terms and ask whether a permit is needed, who may pull it and what inspections follow. Ask how long scheduling takes and what the fee is.

Write down the name of the person you spoke to, the date and the answer. If a contractor disagrees with what the office said, the written record helps. If you hire a plumber, ask them to confirm the same information in writing.

Next Steps

A Practical Rule Of Thumb

If the work only swaps like for like, in the same place, with no change in capacity, fuel or venting, it may be exempt. If it adds, moves or redesigns plumbing, or involves gas, assume a permit is needed. When you are unsure, call the inspections office and ask.

Do not rely on a contractor's word alone. Ask for the permit number, keep the inspection record and file it with your closing papers. It is a small habit that pays off when you sell or file a claim.
