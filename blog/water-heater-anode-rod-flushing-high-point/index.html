Anode Rods And Flushing For High Point Water Heaters

Two cheap maintenance jobs, a yearly flush and an anode rod check, do more for a tank heater than anything else.

The Two Jobs

Flush The Tank Yearly, Check The Rod Every Few Years

A tank water heater has a steel shell, a glass lining and a sacrificial anode rod. The rod corrodes so the tank does not. Sediment settles on the bottom and forces the heater to work harder. According to most manufacturer guidelines, you should flush the tank about once a year, and an anode rod check every few years is common advice.

Both are within reach of a careful homeowner, but they involve hot water, power or gas and heavy parts. If you are unsure at any step, stop and call a plumber.

Safety First

What To Do Before You Touch The Water Heater

Hot water and electricity or gas make shortcuts dangerous.

  1. 1

    Turn off power at the breaker for an electric heater, or set a gas heater's control to pilot or off.

  2. 2

    Close the cold water supply valve above the tank.

  3. 3

    Wait for the water to cool, since tank water can scald.

  4. 4

    Attach a hose to the drain valve and run it to a floor drain or outdoors.

  5. 5

    Open a hot tap in the house so air can enter as the tank drains.

  6. 6

    If you smell gas at any time, leave the house and call 911 or the gas utility.

Flushing

How To Flush A Tank Water Heater

  1. 1

    Drain

    Open the drain valve and let the tank empty into the hose.

  2. 2

    Flush

    Briefly open the cold supply to stir the sediment, then drain again.

  3. 3

    Close up

    Close the drain valve, remove the hose and refill with the hot tap open.

  4. 4

    Restore

    When water runs steadily, close the tap and turn power or gas back on.

Soft Water

What Soft Water Changes About Maintenance

The City of High Point's published hardness readings run roughly 30 to 60 mg/L, which is soft. Scale builds slowly in that water, so the main reason to flush here is fine sediment from the supply, not limestone. A tank in a soft-water area can sometimes go longer between flushes, but a yearly check is still a good habit.

Soft water does not make the anode rod unnecessary. The rod still protects the tank, and a spent rod is one of the main reasons tanks fail. A rotten egg smell in hot water often points to a rod and bacteria reaction and can be fixed with a flush and a rod change.

Know The Limits

When To Call A Plumber Instead

A rod can seize in the tank and need an impact wrench, and removing it needs headroom above the heater. Garage and crawlspace heaters in older houses often lack that space. A drain valve that clogs with sediment can also resist a flush.

If the heater is past about 12 years old, a leak or rust at the bottom is a sign to stop maintenance and price a new unit. Read emergency water heater repair for common failures, and water heater replacement if the tank is done. Owners of on-demand units should see tankless water heater service, since those need a different flush.

Tools

Tools And Time You Will Need

A flush needs a garden hose, a bucket, a screwdriver or valve key for the drain, and about an hour. An anode rod swap needs a large socket, often one and one-sixteenth inches, a breaker bar, thread tape and clearance above the heater to pull the rod out. Flexible rods help where headroom is tight.

If the socket will not budge the rod, stop. Forcing it can damage the tank fittings, and a seized rod is a good reason to call a plumber.

Avoid These

Common Maintenance Mistakes

The most common error is leaving the power on while the tank is empty, which burns out electric elements in seconds. Others are forgetting to open a hot tap so the tank can drain, restoring power before the tank is full and over-tightening the drain valve until it cracks.

A gas heater needs its control set to pilot or off during service. Never relight a pilot if you smell gas. Reusing old thread tape or skipping it on the rod threads invites a slow leak.

Judging The Rod

How To Tell A Spent Anode Rod

A healthy rod is thick and mostly intact. A spent rod looks thin, pitted or reduced to the steel core wire, often with chunks missing. If more than half the coating is gone, or the core wire shows over a long stretch, replace it.

Check the rod about every three to five years, sooner if you notice a sulfur smell. A new rod is far cheaper than a new tank, and it is one of the few maintenance jobs that clearly extends life.

Next Steps

A Maintenance Schedule You Can Keep

Flush the tank once a year and put the date on a label on the heater. Check the anode rod every three to five years, and sooner if the water smells. Test the relief valve gently each year by lifting the lever briefly.

Add a note when the heater reaches ten years. At that point, start comparing replacement options so that a failure does not force a rushed choice.

Your Water

Ask The City Before You Choose An Anode Rod

Anode rod choice depends on water chemistry. The City of High Point's 2025 report lists the lakes, the Ward Water Plant and test results, but it does not state a hardness figure. The Water Resources Department answers questions about the report at (336) 883-3166.

Ask them what is known for your area, and test the tap yourself if you want a number. A tank you cannot flush safely belongs with a plumber, and water heater repair covers worn rods and drain valves. Owners in Trinity and Archdale should ask their own utility.
