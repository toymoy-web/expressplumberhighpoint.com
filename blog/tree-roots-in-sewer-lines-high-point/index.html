Tree Roots And Sewer Lines In High Point

Roots do not break healthy pipes. They find the small cracks and loose joints in old ones, then grow until the line clogs.

How It Works

Roots Look For Water And Find Your Pipe

A sewer line carries warm, nutrient-rich water, and that makes it attractive to roots. Roots enter through tiny cracks, loose joints or poor connections, then grow inside the pipe, catching paper and waste. Over months the line narrows and slows, until a clog appears.

The fix depends on the pipe. In sound pipe, cutting or jetting the roots may work for a year or two. In clay or cracked pipe, the roots will return until the opening is repaired. A camera inspection shows which situation you have.

Warning Signs

Signs Roots Are In Your Sewer Line

Gurgling toilets

Air pushed back by a restriction.

Slow drains in several fixtures

The main line is narrowing.

Repeat clogs every few months

Roots regrow after cabling.

Sewage smell outside

A crack is leaking at the roots.

Greener grass over the line

Leaking wastewater feeds the lawn.

Sinkholes or soft ground

Soil washing into a broken pipe.

Older Neighborhoods

Why Older Triad Neighborhoods See More Root Problems

Clay tile laterals are common under houses built before the 1960s, and each short section means another joint for roots to find. Tree-lined streets such as those in Emerywood, in the Uptown Suburbs historic district, give mature roots decades to reach the pipe. Older cast-iron lines can also scale and crack.

Jamestown's mill village and Archdale's clay-soil neighborhoods see the same pattern. In clay, soil swelling and shrinking flexes pipe joints, opening them a little more each season.

Fixes

Cutting Roots Versus Fixing The Pipe

Cutting and jetting

  • Clears the pipe quickly
  • Costs less now
  • Roots usually return in a year or two
  • Best for sound pipe with minor entry

Repairing or relining

  • Seals the entry points
  • Costs more now
  • Stops regrowth at that spot
  • Best for cracked or open-joint pipe

Related Services

Repair And Replacement Options

A line with one damaged section may be a candidate for sewer lateral repair. A line that is cracked end to end calls for sewer line replacement. Homeowners in Jamestown and other older neighborhoods use the same process.

Long-Term Care

Keeping Roots Out After The Repair

After a repair, schedule a camera check every few years for a line that has had roots. Catching new growth early is cheaper than clearing a full blockage. Keep a record of the footage so a plumber can compare it over time.

Building drains need accessible cleanouts under the International Plumbing Code (IPC), which gives a plumber a way in for cameras. When planting, keep fast-growing, water-seeking trees well away from the line and cleanout, and choose species with less aggressive roots. A root barrier can help in some places, but it needs correct installation to work.

Whose Tree

When The Tree Belongs To Someone Else

Roots can come from a neighbor's tree or a tree in the public right-of-way. Responsibility for damage depends on the facts and local rules, and it is often not simple. If you suspect a city tree, contact the town or city public works office and ask what its process is.

Document the problem with camera footage and dates. A calm conversation with a neighbor goes a long way, since the root zone may cross property lines.

Reading The Evidence

What A Good Camera Report Shows You

A camera inspection should show the whole length of the line, with notes on the distance from the cleanout to each problem. Look for cracks, offset joints, sags where water pools, roots and the pipe material. The plumber should be able to describe each finding and what it means.

Ask for the video or screenshots. If the report calls for a major repair, a second opinion is reasonable. A line with roots at a single joint needs a different repair than one with a collapsed section.

Early Signs

Catching Root Trouble Before A Backup

Slow drains that clear and return, a gurgle in a tub when the toilet flushes and a faint sewer smell near the cleanout all precede a full backup. Do not wait for sewage in the house to act.

If the line is clay or cast iron and the house is older, a camera check every few years is a sensible habit. It costs far less than an emergency call at night, and it shows where trouble is forming.

Next Steps

A Sensible Plan

If roots are in your line, get a camera inspection, clear the roots and decide on repair based on what the footage shows. Plan follow-up cleaning if the line is sound but roots recur. Keep records of each service.

If you are planting or replacing trees, choose species and locations that keep roots away from the line. A small decision in the yard can save thousands of dollars underground.

Local Pattern

Where Roots Show Up Most In The Area

Older streets with mature canopy, such as the Uptown Suburbs and Emerywood in High Point and the mill-village streets of Jamestown, produce the most calls. Clay soil in Archdale adds movement that opens joints. Newer subdivisions with young trees tend to see problems ten to twenty years later as the trees mature.

Before You Dig

Call 811 Before Any Sewer Repair Dig In High Point

NC811 says to contact 811 or 1-800-632-4949 three working days before you plan to dig, and that utilities mark their publicly managed lines for free. A sewer repair that opens the yard needs that call. Private lines such as irrigation or a shed circuit are generally not marked, so tell the crew what you know.

Ask the City of High Point which part of the sewer connection it maintains before you pay for a repair, because the answer decides who handles which section. Roots are the usual reason a line fills again, and a camera view guides sewer line repair. Owners in Jamestown and Thomasville can ask their own utilities the same question.
