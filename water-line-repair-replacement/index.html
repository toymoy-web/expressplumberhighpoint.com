Which Pipe

The Water Service Line: From The Meter To Your House

The water service line is the buried pipe that carries city water from the meter to your house. Inside the house, supply lines are a different system, covered under repiping service. A leak of one gallon a minute in the buried line wastes 1,440 gallons a day.

Customers generally own the pipe on their side of the meter, while the utility owns the meter and the main. Confirm the boundary with City of High Point Public Services before digging.

A service line leak is easy to confuse with an in-house leak. A simple test separates them: close the main valve at the house and watch the meter. If it keeps moving, the leak is outside, in the buried line.

Emergency Water Line

Emergency Water Line Repair: No Water Or Water Pouring Out

A service line break is an emergency when the yard is flooding, the house has lost pressure or there is no water at all. Close the house-side valve if you can reach it. If water keeps flowing, the leak is on the supply side before that valve, so call the emergency line and the water utility.

Tell the dispatcher where the water is surfacing and whether anyone has already dug. A line that a utility crew has uncovered can often be repaired the same visit, because the exposed pipe saves the digging time. Do not dig near a buried line without calling 811 first.

Pipe Materials

What Service Lines Are Made Of

Older High Point houses may have galvanized steel service lines, which rust from the inside and slowly choke the flow. Copper is common in mid-century homes. Newer houses typically use polyethylene or PEX. A line built of one material is often joined to another at the meter or the house, and those joints are frequent failure points.

Federal rules required water utilities to inventory the material of service lines by October 2024, in part to find lead pipe. Ask City Public Services whether your line is on that inventory, and if you have any doubt about lead, have a plumber identify the material.

Digging Safely

What To Do Before Anyone Digs

Buried utilities make a careless dig dangerous and expensive.

  1. 1

    Call 811, the NC811 service, a few days before any digging so gas, power and communications lines can be marked.

  2. 2

    Find your meter box and main shutoff valve, and clear access to them.

  3. 3

    Do not dig near the suspected leak yourself, because saturated ground can collapse.

  4. 4

    Mark where you noticed wet soil, a sinkhole or a dead patch of grass.

  5. 5

    Keep children and pets away from any open trench.

Local Ground

What Digging Looks Like In This Area

The Piedmont's red clay compacts hard in dry weather and turns slick in wet, and mature trees along older streets such as those in Emerywood send roots across service line routes. In the older Uptown Suburbs, the line may cross a historic streetscape that you do not want torn up, which is a good reason to ask about trenchless replacement.

Pressure matters as well. A pressure reducing valve near the house protects fixtures from the city's supply pressure, and a failed valve can add stress to an old line. Ask the plumber to read the pressure at an outdoor spigot before and after any service line work.

Quotes And Replacements

Replacing The Line From The Meter To The House

Many callers are not chasing a leak. They already know the old line is failing and want a price to replace it from the meter to the house. A useful quote names the pipe material, the route, the depth, how the yard and driveway are restored and who handles the permit.

Ask whether the quote covers the connection at the meter and at the house, a new shutoff valve and a pressure test. Trenchless methods can limit digging on a finished lawn, while open trenches are easier to inspect. Compare quotes on those details, not only on the total.

Older Pipe And Lead Questions

Old Service Lines, Lead Questions And Utility-Found Leaks

The City of High Point states that lead pipes are more likely in homes built before 1986 and has started an inventory of service lines. Homeowners who do not know what their line is made of can call the City Customer Service Center at (336) 883-3111 or use the city's lead-free service line page to see how it classifies the property.

Sometimes a utility crew finds a leak while working near your property and leaves it exposed. Call the emergency line, tell the dispatcher what the crew said and keep the crew's contact details, since the repair may depend on who owns that section of pipe.

Low Pressure Or No Water

Low Water Pressure And No Water At The House

Weak pressure that affects every fixture often points to the service line, the pressure regulator or a partly closed valve. Pressure that is low at one fixture usually means a clogged aerator or cartridge. Houses on a private well add the pressure tank and pump to the list.

A house that has no water at all should check the main valve, the meter box and whether neighbors have water. If the street is dry, the issue belongs to the utility. If only your house is dry, a plumber can test the line and the valve before anyone digs.
