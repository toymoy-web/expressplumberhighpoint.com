Emergency Toilet Repair

When A Toilet Problem Is An Emergency

A toilet is an emergency when it is overflowing, leaking onto the floor, cracked or backing up with other drains. Turn the supply valve behind the toilet clockwise to stop new water. If several fixtures gurgle or back up together, the trouble is in the main drain, not the toilet, and the emergency line should be called right away.

Do not keep flushing an overflowing bowl. Lift the tank lid and press the flapper down to stop the refill, then dry the floor. If the toilet is cracked or the base is leaking, shut the supply and wait for the plumber.

DIY Check

What To Do Before Calling About A Running Toilet

A running toilet wastes water around the clock. These checks narrow down the problem.

  1. 1

    Add a few drops of food coloring to the tank and wait 10 minutes without flushing. If color shows in the bowl, the flapper leaks.

  2. 2

    Lift the tank lid and look at the water level. It should sit about an inch below the overflow tube.

  3. 3

    Check that the chain has a little slack and is not caught under the flapper.

  4. 4

    Feel around the base and supply line for dampness after a flush.

  5. 5

    Note the age of the toilet. Older ones often have tanks that no longer match modern repair parts.

Water Waste

A Running Toilet Is A Costly Leak

The EPA's WaterSense program notes that toilets are among the most common sources of household leaks. A flapper that leaks slowly can waste thousands of gallons a year without making any noise. Replacing worn parts is cheap compared to the water bill it can create.

Replacing an old toilet can also save water. WaterSense labeled toilets use 1.28 gallons or less per flush, compared with the older 3.5 or more gallons of toilets from before the 1990s. In older High Point houses, an original toilet may be the single biggest daily water user.

Decision

Repair Or Replace A Toilet

Repairing makes sense when

  • The toilet is not cracked
  • The problem is a flapper, fill valve or wax ring
  • The bowl and tank are in good shape
  • Flushing performance is acceptable

Replacing makes sense when

  • The bowl or tank is cracked
  • It clogs often or flushes weakly
  • Parts are no longer available
  • You want lower water use or comfort height
Installation

How A Toilet Replacement Goes

  1. 1

    Measure

    The rough-in, the distance from wall to flange center, is checked, commonly 12 inches.

  2. 2

    Remove

    Water is shut off, the old toilet is drained and lifted, and the flange is inspected.

  3. 3

    Set

    A new wax ring or gasket and a fresh bolt set seal the new toilet.

  4. 4

    Connect and test

    The supply line is connected, the tank is filled and the seal is leak tested.

Older Homes

Toilet Floors And Flanges In Older High Point Houses

In houses from the Uptown Suburbs district and the mill houses near Highland Cotton Mills, bathroom floors sit over crawlspaces. A toilet that has leaked at the base for years can soften the subfloor, and the cast-iron or lead-and-oakum flange underneath may be corroded. A good installer checks the flange and floor before setting a new toilet.

If the toilet keeps clogging despite a clean bowl, the problem is often further down the drain, and drain cleaning is the solution. A leak at the supply line or a dripping tub spout points to faucet and sink repair in the same visit.

Parts

Wax Rings, Flush Levers And Bidet Seats

A wobbly toilet or water at the base usually means a failed wax ring or a loose closet bolt. A broken flush lever is a quick repair, which matters for someone who struggles with a stiff handle. Bidet seats and washlets add electrical and supply connections, so ask for a plumber who has installed them.

A replacement quote should say whether it includes removing the old toilet, a new wax ring, a supply line and a new shutoff valve. A one-piece toilet is heavy, so ask who carries it.
